Signal Temporal Logic describes properties about what a signal should do over time, and it produces a robustness which is just a signed number about how well a trace satisfied that property. We'll look at how to actually calculate robustness given a signal and a handful of formulas.

The signal is speed and we take five samples that are measured every second.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## Predicates

The smallest formula compares a term against a constant. Its robustness is the signed distance to the boundary: `f(x) - c` for `>` and `>=`, and `c - f(x)` for `<` and `<=`.

Take `speed > 5` at $t = 0$. The signal reads 12, so robustness is $12 - 5 = 7$. The property holds with seven units to spare. Now take `speed < 10` at $t = 0$. That scores $10 - 12 = -2$, a violation, because the signal starts two units above the ceiling.

Evaluated across the whole trace, `speed > 5` produces a robustness signal, one value per sample:

| $t$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `speed - 5` | 7 | 4 | 2 | -1 | 1 |

The dip to $-1$ at $t = 3$ is the only violation.

## Boolean connectives

Once we solve for the predicates and get a number, the boolean operators then work on numbers. Conjunction takes the minimum, disjunction the maximum, negation flips the sign, and implication, `phi -> psi`, is `max(-rho_phi, rho_psi)`.

Conjunction is the minimum because a formula is only as satisfied as its weakest part. Consider `(speed > 5) & (speed < 10)` at $t = 0$. The first conjunct scores $7$, the second $-2$, and the minimum is $-2$. The conjunction fails, and it fails by the amount the ceiling is breached.

## G is the worst case over a window

`G[a, b] phi`, read always, says phi holds at every point from $t + a$ to $t + b$. Its robustness is the infimum of the inner robustness over that window.

$$
\rho(\mathbf{G}_{[a,b]}\, \varphi, x, t) = \inf_{s \in [t+a,\, t+b]} \rho(\varphi, x, s)
$$

Unbounded, `G (speed > 5)` takes the infimum of $\{7, 4, 2, -1, 1\}$, which is $-1$ at $t = 3$. That $-1$ sets the verdict for the whole trace. If we relax the threshold, the worst case improves. `G (speed > 3)` scores $\{9, 6, 4, 1, 3\}$ and its infimum is $1$: the property holds, with the tightest moment again at $t = 3$ where the margin is one.

Bound the window and only the samples inside it count. `G[0, 2] (speed > 5)` at $t = 0$ looks at $t = 0, 1, 2$, the values $\{7, 4, 2\}$, and reports $2$. The violation at $t = 3$ sits outside the window, so within the first two seconds the property holds comfortably. The same `G[0, 2] (speed > 5)` at $t = 1$ looks at $t = 1, 2, 3$, the values $\{4, 2, -1\}$, and reports $-1$. In this case, the violation is part of the considered values so it doesn't hold.

## F is the best case over a window

`F[a, b] phi`, read eventually says phi holds at some point from $t + a$ to $t + b$. Its robustness is the supremum of the inner robustness over that window.

$$
\rho(\mathbf{F}_{[a,b]}\, \varphi, x, t) = \sup_{s \in [t+a,\, t+b]} \rho(\varphi, x, s)
$$

`F (speed > 10)` scores the predicate `speed - 10` across the trace, $\{2, -1, -3, -6, -4\}$, and takes the supremum, $2$ at $t = 0$. The speed climbs above ten at exactly one instant, and that one instant is enough to satisfy an `F`.

## Until

`U`, read until, is the one binary temporal operator. `phi1 U[a, b] phi2` wants a witness time where `phi2` holds, with `phi1` maintained the whole way there.

$$
\rho(\varphi_1\, \mathbf{U}_{[a,b]}\, \varphi_2, x, t) = \sup_{s \in [t+a,\, t+b]} \min\!\left( \rho(\varphi_2, x, s),\; \inf_{r \in [t,\, s]} \rho(\varphi_1, x, r) \right)
$$

Read `(speed > 5) U (speed < 8)` at $t = 0$. The first predicate scores $\{7, 4, 2, -1, 1\}$ and the second, `8 - speed`, scores $\{-4, -1, 1, 4, 2\}$. The best witness is $t = 2$: there `speed < 8` holds with margin $1$, and `speed > 5` held with margin at least $2$ over $t = 0, 1, 2$, so the witness contributes $\min(1, 2) = 1$. Later witnesses look tempting on `speed < 8` alone, but by $t = 3$ the speed has already broken the `speed > 5` guard, which caps their contribution at $-1$. The supremum over all witnesses is $1$, so the property holds.
